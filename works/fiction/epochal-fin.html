Epochal

by Sydney Wentz · 2026

She wakes up in warmth. She doesn’t remember much, but there wasn’t anything to remember in the first place; only darkness and a deep and terrible chill that rattles her bones as she lays there, staring at the objects spinning above her.

She begins to cry.

A hum floats from the hallway into the room, reaching into the crib she lies in and soothing her ears.

“Oh, Emma, what’s the matter?” the voice says. A woman enters the room, her face framed by wild, curly hair, with her arms outstretched. She grabs her from the crib and holds her to her chest, bouncing them both up and down. “You wake up grumpy, honey?” the woman says, her voice thick with sugar.

Emma’s cries peter out as she becomes entranced with the woman. She is sunlight and stardust; a familiar feeling Emma can’t quite place. They bounce in the nursery until Emma’s head falls against the woman’s chest, gentle snoring parting her lips.

Emma lived her life here in this house with this woman, who later became Mom, playing soccer, dirtying brand-new shoes with mud in the creek, and learning the art of photography. She left for college, returned for when her mother began dying, and stayed well after her mother passed. Only a week later, she bumped into a man on a random sidewalk while she was taking pictures in the city. She only saw him coming when the light glinted off of his hair, a blazing bronze color. She learned, over coffee, that his name was Ermin, but he liked to go by ‘Ermie’ and that he was a journalist. Soon after, Ermie was offered a gig touring the world and so she packed her things and followed him through countless countries, becoming a renowned photojournalist herself. They saw–firsthand– death and destruction, loss, and the pure strength of community. They followed soldiers through wars, towns through famine, rebellions and revolutions. They married in Japan, had children in Spain, their first home in Brazil, and yet–despite all their adventures–they settled back into that house Emma grew up in.

Here, they lived out their days until Emma began to grow weak from the same disease that took her mother all those years ago. Surrounded by her three grown children, Ermie, and somewhere, the spirit of her mother, Emma passed in her home, in a hospital bed her husband demanded be brought into their bedroom.

As soon as she closed her eyes for that last time, she fell into darkness, a bitter cold forcing its way around her.

She woke up in warmth, again. Sunlight licked at her face, the branch she perched upon shaking in the breeze. Out on the marsh below her, her flock wade in the brackish water, peeking below the surface for fish and mollusks.

Days must pass with her in this tree. She pecks at the bugs that crawl from their burrows in the branch below her for sustenance but mostly, she just watches the water. This morning, with the sun hanging above her for longer than usual, a booming voice approaches her oasis.

“Here, you’ll see the wood stork, native to our wetlands here in Georgia. Right now, they seem to be feeding.”

A group of people gaggle around the edge of the marsh, binoculars held to their eyes, mouths open.

“Their diet consists of fish, amphibians, insects, and snails. This flock actually was here before this conservation program was, all of them born and raised here, except for the black one you’ll see up in the tree to your right.” The man with the speaker at the front of the group points to her. The group turn their attention fully to her. “She would be our only black stork, typically found in Asia and Europe.” “Well, then how did she end up here, Ermie?” a child calls from the front of the group. His mother shushes him, ruffling his hair. Ermie.

She knows that name. “Good question, kiddo! She was brought to us last year as a rescue from an exotic breeder. Thankfully, the flock took her in as their own. She is not very active compared to the other members of her flock, but she seems to enjoy it here.” She cocks her head, her long beak sticking out in front of her. She doesn’t remember that. “We call her Emma.”

The name moves through the crowd as they murmur it. The man smiles and begins to walk backward, waving his hands toward his chest.

“If you’ll follow me, we will move onto our next flock, the Great Egrets.”

The group turns all at once and shuffle after him. She watches them go.

Emma.

She knows that name, too. It sticks to her brain, feeling so familiar yet so unknown.

Her flock startles from a noise far into the woods behind them, flapping into her tree to settle in its branches.

Ermie.

Why does she know that name? Her feet are glued to her branch, her toes gripping the wood.

Emma.

She goes to say it and all that comes out is a discordant noise that begins to echo through the tree, the rest of the flock honking in response.

Ermie.

Before she realizes it, years pass with her sitting in the tree; her flock bringing her bugs and frogs every so often, so she’ll feed.

Emma, Emma, Emma. Tours dwindle in size and frequency throughout these years but with every one, the guide points her out, her oddity in species, habits, and name. One early walk around the conservation property, one of the guides finds Emma at rest in the mud right below her branch. She alerts the foundation and soon, Emma’s story becomes one of past tense. Protocol is to let nature take its course on passed wildlife and so her body grows over with fungi and moss, decaying into the soil. Predators peck on her corpse, prey crawling over it and months later, there is no trace of Emma except for the plaque hammered into the ground in front of the tree that says, Home to Emma’s Branch.

She floats in that coldness until the last of her tissue and bone are broken into pieces of ammonia for the decomposers to nitrify.

She wakes up in warmth. Red and yellow leaves shiver from where they sprout from her bark. Rolling hills and green pastures fill her vision, wildflowers and bumblebees swaying with each other. It’s quiet out here. The chirps of birds and skitters of squirrels are the only sounds that fill the space. For decades, she stands mighty from the ground, branches reaching for the sun, seeing nothing of humanity as she grows. One day, when the sun was a little whiter and the air a little cooler, a young man comes along. In his arms he carries a woven basket and a blanket. He throws the gingham print out at her trunk and rifles through the contents of the basket. He sits there for hours, chewing on apple after apple, before packing his things and trekking off. The next week, he returns. Every week since that very first day, he treks to her base and lays out. She can see him coming from the bottom of the hill she sprouts from by the sheer light that reflects off of his deep yellow hair. Season through season she sees him, always alone. And though they may not communicate, something draws her toward him, and surely, him toward her. Years upon years upon years into this routine, he doesn’t show up for their time together. A month goes by without him and one day a woman with his eyes and bronze hair that burns like fire makes her way to Emma.

At Emma’s trunk, she digs a small hole and places a metal urn inside it before covering it with soil. On top of the earth now disturbed, she sticks a cross with the name “Ermie” written on it with a heart. She sees another century or two, maybe three, of passing day and night and nothing of humanity as she fights storms and frost. On one of her completed travels on the Earth around the sun, a burly man dressed in a flannel cuts her down.
